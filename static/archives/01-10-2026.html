The top secret URSALA, RAQUEL, and FARRAH satellites (2025)

224 points • by Bluestein • 12 comments
Summary

Between 1971‑1986 the U.S. launched 20 low‑Earth‑orbit “hitchhiker” subsatellites from the side of HEXAGON photo‑reconnaissance spacecraft. Initially designated Program 11, then P‑770B and Program 989, these small ELINT platforms (often called Mission 7300) intercepted radar and communications signals, recording them for later downlink or retransmission. By the 1970s the NRO standardized the designs, creating the URSALA, RAQUEL and later FARRAH families.

  • URSALA (four launches 1972‑1979) performed general‑search ELINT in 2‑12 GHz, detecting radar side‑lobes and supporting the Tactical Exploitation of National Capabilities (TENCAP) concept. Mass 178–259 kg, design lifetimes 9‑18 months, actual operations lasted 35‑133 months; later units added encrypted downlinks for field vans.
  • RAQUEL (two launches 1974, 1978) targeted 4‑18 GHz main‑beam signals with a directed‑search payload, providing fine‑grained technical intelligence. Mass ≈256 kg, design life 18 months, but operated up to 63 months; supported operations in the Falklands War and monitored Soviet activity in Afghanistan.
  • FARRAH (four launches 1982‑1984, later) combined URSALA’s general‑search and RAQUEL’s technical‑intelligence functions, covering 2‑18 GHz. Mass ≈340 kg, 36‑month design life, exceeded expectations; equipped with an on‑board CDC‑469 computer for real‑time de‑interleaving and direct transmission to tactical ground stations.

These subsatellites extended U.S. SIGINT from strategic national users to Army, Navy and Air Force units worldwide, enabling near‑real‑time electronic order‑of‑battle data in multiple conflicts.

Why the Bronze Age Collapsed

246 points • by AnodicElegy • 31 comments
Summary

The article argues that the Bronze Age collapse (c. 1200 BC) resulted not only from environmental stresses, Sea Peoples, and trade disruptions but primarily from the transition to iron weaponry. Bronze, an alloy of copper and scarce tin sourced from distant mines (Afghanistan, Cornwall, etc.), concentrated military power in elite, centralized states such as Egypt, Hittite, Assyrian, Babylonian, and Mycenaean polities. When iron smelting via bloomery furnaces emerged (≈ 1400 BC, possibly in India or the Caucasus), iron became locally obtainable, cheap, and abundant, democratizing access to weapons. This eroded the strategic advantage of imperial cores, leading to increased internal violence, shorter reigns, and a failure of large‑scale empire reformation for centuries. Archaeological evidence—destruction layers at Troy and Greek palaces, a rise in trauma‑related skeletal injuries, and a surge in recorded battles—supports heightened conflict after iron adoption. Consequently, the Iron Age saw fragmented polities, the rise of new institutions (Greek democracy, Phoenician commerce, Abrahamic religions), and a lasting shift in the balance of power driven by accessible military technology.
