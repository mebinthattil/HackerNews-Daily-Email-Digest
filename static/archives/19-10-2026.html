William Gibson and the trademarking of cyberspace

176 points • by Eric_Gullichsen • 10 comments
Summary

Autodesk’s research lab applied for a “cyberspace” trademark in the late 1980s while developing an early virtual‑reality system (386 PC, dual Matrox cards, VPL EyePhone, Polhemus tracker, data glove, 6‑10 fps, a few hundred polygons). Timothy Leary, a friend of the author and fan of William Gibson’s Neuromancer, nicknamed the lab “Cyberia” and later circulated a rumor that Gibson was retaliating by trying to trademark “Eric Gullichsen”. Gibson’s lawyers did send Autodesk a letter objecting to the “cyberspace” filing, but Autodesk ignored it. The “Eric Gullichsen” claim was a prank by Leary, later amplified by others. At Art Futura 1990 in Barcelona, Gibson met the author and remarked that “Autodesk is not the street,” confirming his objection but indicating no further trademark action. Thus, Autodesk did seek the “cyberspace” trademark; Gibson objected; the alleged Gibson retaliation trademark is a myth.

How to Train Your Robot

17 points • by crescit_eundo • 2 comments
Summary

The recent surge in AI performance is attributed to the “scaling hypothesis”: larger models, larger datasets, and more compute yield higher intelligence. While language models have access to hundreds of trillions of text tokens, robotics lacks comparable public data. Training robots requires massive examples of correct actions, obtained through several methods:

  • Teleoperation (behavioral cloning) – humans control robots, recording state and visual data; high fidelity but expensive and limited in dexterity.
  • Reinforcement learning on real robots – autonomous robots collect success/failure scores; promising “data flywheel” but currently sparse and repetitive.
  • Simulation – cheap, fast generation of billions of steps; effective for locomotion, limited for manipulation due to the sim‑to‑real gap. Techniques like domain randomization and Automatic Domain Randomization expand variability.
  • Synthetic data – converting teleoperation recordings into simulated variants.
  • Human‑collected video (egocentric, UMI, tactile gloves) – cheap, diverse; requires mapping to robot actions and suffers from imperfect robot‑human correspondence.

Existing robotic datasets range from 1 M to >10 M task trajectories (≈4 k–10 k h) to tens of thousands of hours of UMI video. Compared to LLMs (GPT‑4 ≈ 685 M h equivalent), robotics faces a “100 k‑year data gap.” Rough cost estimates for closing this gap span $10–100 B. Researchers anticipate that scaling, improved sample efficiency, or leveraging generalist AI may eventually bridge the gap, but the exact data requirements remain uncertain.

Superlite: A Faster Alternatie to Superpowers

92 points • by iheatuchriswogu • 25 comments
Summary

Superlite is a Claude Code plugin that automates end‑to‑end software development from a single specification. It replaces the highly interactive “superpowers” workflow with a single approval, then generates a parallel task graph, assigns each task to the cheapest capable sub‑agent, runs tasks concurrently, and performs automated seam checks and reviews. Core components include: Opus (orchestrator, never edits code), Sonnet (primary builder), Haiku (small mechanical tasks), dedicated review agents, and tiered fix‑only agents. Hooks enforce rules (no coordinator edits, background test execution, lint stability) and record token usage, cost, and time for budgeting. Benchmarks on a static‑site‑generator + spreadsheet engine project (177 hidden tests) show Superlite completing builds in 16.1 minutes—about half the time of the prior Superpowers setup—by reducing human gates, eliminating strict sequential dispatch, and moving test runs to background agents. Installation requires Claude Code, git, Python 3.10+, and Bash; usage involves providing a spec, optionally choosing a human or AI orchestrator, and monitoring progress via generated logs (progress.md, test-summary.json, metrics.jsonl). The plugin is open‑source on GitHub (iha2/superlite).
