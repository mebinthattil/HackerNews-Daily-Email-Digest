Energy-Based Reasoners: 385x more efficient inference

Summary

Energy-Based Reasoners (EBRs) extend looped recurrent models by learning an explicit energy function over latent reasoning states. The energy scores compatibility between a state and the problem input; its gradient directs iterative refinement via gradient descent, and a small gradient norm serves as an adaptive stopping criterion. Training incorporates exploration to expose additional modes and shape the energy landscape across diverse states. Empirically, EBRs match or exceed prior recurrent reasoners on four algorithmic reasoning benchmarks, achieving 99.9 % accuracy on Sudoku‑Extreme while using 345× fewer inference FLOPs than the next best model and comparable training compute. Adaptive stopping allows easy puzzles to finish in few steps, and scaling the number of descent steps or trajectories improves solution quality. On multi‑solution tasks, EBRs attain state‑of‑the‑art coverage of distinct valid solutions, maintaining near‑100 % single‑sample accuracy. These results demonstrate that energy‑based formulations provide explicit progress signals, efficient inference, and strong performance for complex reasoning problems.

Java4Shell: RCE in OpenJDK when processing Base64

983 points • by werrett • 66 comments
Summary

The write‑up details Java4Shell, a memory‑corruption bug in OpenJDK HotSpot’s x86‑64 Base64 intrinsic. The intrinsic miscalculates the mask for the final padded block, causing evmovdqub to write 64 bytes with a mask of ‑1 u64. When decoding a malformed input such as 64 ‘A’s followed by ‘=’, the decoder allocates a 50‑byte buffer but writes past its end (offset 48 to 111), yielding a controlled out‑of‑bounds write.

Exploitation flow
- Warm‑up the Base64 decoder until C2 JIT compiles the intrinsic.
- Trigger the overflow to overwrite a Java array length (the “writer”), gaining a bounded arbitrary write.
- Use the corrupted length to corrupt a neighboring array (the “reader”), creating a heap‑leak primitive (JavaBleed).
- Perform heap‑Feng‑Shui (spray, TLAB manipulation) to align objects, then extend writes to the Code Cache and JVM hooks for full remote code execution.

Affected releases: OpenJDK 17.0.6+ on x86‑64 CPUs with AVX‑512 VBMI; all downstream builds (Amazon Corretto, Eclipse Temurin, Microsoft, Azul, BellSoft, SAP, Red Hat, Oracle, GraalVM).

Mitigations: update to patched builds (released Nov 17 2026) or disable the intrinsic (-XX:-UseBASE64Intrinsics) or AVX‑512 (-XX:UseAVX=2).

Google's AI answers reducing search traffic by 40%

459 points • by slashdev • 74 comments
Summary

Google’s AI “Overviews” now appear on >60 % of U.S. English queries (up from ~6 % in 2022) and sit above organic results, linking to cited sources. A randomized Chrome‑extension experiment (Jan–Feb 2026) showed that when Overviews are visible, users click external sites 0.37 times per query versus 0.62 without them—a 39.8 % drop; the proportion of searches ending with no outbound click rose from 54 % to 73 %. Overall organic clicks to publishers are estimated to fall ~18.5 % and the CTR of the top organic result dropped 58 % on desktop (17.8 %→7.4 %) between Sep 2025 and Aug 2026.

Citation analysis indicates that domains with more referring domains are moderately more likely to be cited (Spearman ≈ 0.4), but size, not specific tactics, drives visibility. “Generative Engine Optimization” (GEO) essentially mirrors SEO: being linked from high‑authority sites, adding quotable data, statistics, and source citations improves citation odds; shortcuts such as llms.txt or AI‑specific markup show no measurable benefit.

AI assistants direct only ~0.3 % of total traffic (≈3.5 M visits in Mar 2026) but exhibit higher conversion rates on the few sites that receive them. Recommended actions: maintain indexable, link‑rich pages; monitor which sources are cited per topic; craft content that can be quoted; avoid unproven shortcuts; and evaluate performance by revenue rather than click volume.

Why is Gemini 4 Carbon taking so long?

126 points • by deenadz • 20 comments
Summary

The post examines why Gemini 4 Carbon (referred to as “Carbon”) spends more time fixing bugs than GPT‑6 Astra. In a baseline trial both models identified a bug’s cause quickly—Astra within a minute, Carbon after about six minutes. Astra wrote a fix three minutes later, whereas Carbon waited roughly 25 minutes before producing code. Two hypotheses are considered: Carbon may have been uncertain of the cause (knowledge gap) or may have continued despite knowing it, reflecting a training bias that rewards correct answers over minimizing steps. To test behavior, a modified harness injected a “do you already have enough to make the fix?” check‑in every five turns. With these prompts, Carbon’s total runtime fell from 37 minutes to 28 minutes, and the lag between cause identification and editing shrank from 25 minutes to ~10 minutes, indicating that the slowdown stems from Carbon’s procedural behavior rather than lack of knowledge.
