Where did all the public bathrooms go?

188 points • by herbertl • 60 comments
Summary

The article traces the rise and decline of public urinals (“pissoirs”) in 19th‑century Paris and their American counterparts. After cholera outbreaks in the 1850s, Paris banned street urination and installed ornate, cast‑iron or slate pissoirs—often single‑stall or multi‑stall units with modesty screens, decorative ironwork, and advertising, sometimes illuminated by streetlamps. They were gender‑specific, serving only men, and quickly became informal meeting spots for clandestine encounters and graffiti. Police patrols and blackmail attempts followed, leading to a crackdown. In the United States, temperance advocates promoted large, multi‑stall “comfort stations” to replace saloon bathrooms, but Prohibition halted new construction and existing facilities fell into disrepair. Consequently, most early‑20th‑century public restrooms disappeared, leaving urban pedestrians reliant on semi‑private venues (hotels, shops, cafés) where restroom access is treated as a purchasable commodity rather than a civic right.
