google.com/goto: Google's anti-scraping update

360 points • by 1e1a • 49 comments
Summary

Google Search now rewrites organic result links to google.com/goto?url=… instead of exposing the destination URL directly in the HTML. The url= parameter uses a proprietary encoding that cannot be decoded offline; the real target URL is provided only in the HTTP Location header of the /goto response, which must be read (e.g., via a HEAD request) without following the redirect. This change, observed consistently from late August 2026 for logged‑out and private sessions, replaces the older google.com/url?q= wrapper and forces each result to require an additional request to Google to obtain the final URL. The shift is part of Google’s broader anti‑scraping measures aimed at increasing the cost and traceability of automated SERP harvesting by AI crawlers and SEO scrapers. In response, Autom has updated its Google Search pipeline to resolve /goto links by reading the Location header and return the final URLs in its API, preserving existing integration behavior.

Retrospectively Reverse-Engineering Apple's Neural Engine

6 points • by zdw • 0 comments
Summary

The article documents a reverse‑engineering effort of the Apple Neural Engine (ANE) used in M1 chips, focusing on its internal architecture rather than trying to make it a general‑purpose accelerator. The ANE comprises 16 compute cores, each with 128 FP16 (or 256 INT8) MAC lanes, giving 2 048 parallel MACs that perform fixed‑point multiply‑accumulate with a 32‑bit accumulator saturating at 2¹⁵. Dot‑product, convolution, and attention operations are distinguished only by data‑mapping and scheduling, while the post‑MAC activation is applied point‑wise via a 33‑entry piecewise‑linear lookup table (mode 2) with linear interpolation and a scaling factor R that controls knot spacing.

The driver does not issue high‑level opcodes; instead it loads a pre‑compiled command stream of fixed‑size task descriptors (TDs) into a task queue (TQ). Each TQ holds status, priority, two descriptor slots, and a 32‑entry BAR table for virtual‑address base relocation. Submission uses TM_ADDR, TM_INFO, and TM_PUSH registers, resembling a GPU push‑buffer. The hardware walks the descriptor stream, executes DMA moves, kernel configuration, and PE operations without dynamic address calculations, indicating a highly static, data‑flow‑oriented execution model. The analysis concludes that ANE’s design reflects early CNN workloads and that later integration of its cores into the GPU marks the end of the standalone NPU.

A Design Space Exploration of Async/Await

259 points • by wcrichton • 25 comments
Summary

The paper “A Design Space Exploration of Async/Await” analyzes straight‑line asynchrony across modern languages (Python, Rust, Swift, C#, JavaScript, Tokio, Smol, Trio). Using a simple pseudocode example—logging in a background task and awaiting it—the authors show that seven runtimes produce four distinct outputs, and no two produce the same result for all three program variants. They attribute this variance to design dimensions that affect observable semantics rather than performance. Nine dimensions are identified and grouped into three lifetime phases: Start of Life (e.g., eager vs. lazy task creation), End of Life (e.g., dynamic vs. indefinite extent, destruction policy such as cancellation or awaiting), and Cancellation (e.g., propagation direction, cancellation awareness). Two dimensions—Dynamic Extent and Destruction—explain why Swift prints “AC” while Trio prints “ABC”. The authors formalize the space with a core calculus and small‑step semantics, highlighting rule choices that cause divergent executions. The work demonstrates that async/await designs are far from uniform, each reflecting trade‑offs in ergonomics, memory, and semantics.
